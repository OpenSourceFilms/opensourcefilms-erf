ERF Stack Landscape Check
This report answers the July 2026 brief against the current ERF stack you specified and cross-checks it against the previously uploaded ERF notes where they materially help interpret the choices. The highest-confidence conclusion is that there is no single 2026 replacement that cleanly collapses your current ASR + alignment + diarization + speaker-ID + face/ASD fusion stack into one clearly better open/self-hosted system. The strongest upgrades are narrower: Parakeet/Canary-class ASR are worth a bake-off against Whisper for raw English transcription on noisy audio; pyannote’s hosted precision-2 is a real diarization upgrade if hosted processing is acceptable; and speaker verification is the area where a fresh bake-off against ReDimNet2 / ECAPA2 / strong SSL-front-end models is most justified. For face recognition, active speaker detection, orchestration, and rendering, your current choices still look fundamentally sensible for a single-operator batch system. 
 

Speech recognition
Noisy English ASR that can credibly challenge faster-whisper large-v3.
Recommendation: run a serious bake-off with NVIDIA Parakeet-TDT-0.6B-v3 as the main challenger. Runner-up: NVIDIA Canary-1B-v2. Why: among the public primary sources I could verify, Parakeet is the cleanest open/self-hosted “better raw ASR” candidate for English production audio, while Canary has the stronger formal claim against Whisper-large-v3 in NVIDIA’s own release materials. By contrast, Whisper large-v3-turbo is best understood as a speed trade, not an accuracy-led replacement for large-v3; and the public-source picture for Voxtral, Kyutai STT, and any clearly released Canary-Qwen variant remained incomplete enough that I would not tell you they are safer bets than your incumbent without a local bake-off. 

For timestamps, the open 2026 landscape still splits into two camps: models that transcribe well, and aligners that produce editor-safe word timings. That matters because your use case is not “good enough subtitle timing”; it is word-exact edit points. NVIDIA’s STT stack is promising enough on raw recognition that it deserves evaluation, but the strongest publicly documented timestamping pipelines are still the ones that either emit explicit word information or are reconciled with an external aligner. That is why I would treat ASR model choice and word-timestamp layer choice as two separate decisions rather than expecting the winning recogniser to solve both. 

On hallucinations over silence/music, the public 2025–2026 primary sources I reviewed do not give you a clean, apples-to-apples official benchmark across Whisper, Parakeet, Canary, Voxtral, and Kyutai on “music / low-speech / silence-only” failure modes. The safest statement is therefore narrower: Gladia explicitly markets “no hallucinations” for its hosted async STT, while the open model cards I reviewed do not give a directly comparable public metric, so this remains something to test locally with your dropout windows and music beds rather than infer from leaderboard numbers. 

Licence, VRAM, maintenance, verdict.
Parakeet and Canary are published through NVIDIA’s model ecosystem and documentation, with active 2025–2026 maintenance signals in those materials. Whisper large-v3 / large-v3-turbo remain active and ubiquitous, but turbo is a throughput-first option. I would budget “single modern GPU” class evaluation for Parakeet/Canary and keep your current Whisper path as the baseline. Switch worth it versus faster-whisper large-v3: only-if you are willing to re-run the full ERF bake-off, because the ASR upside looks real, but the timestamp/editorial safety story is still not obviously superior end-to-end. 

Forced alignment.
Recommendation: keep WhisperX as the default alignment layer for a Whisper-family-centric stack. Runner-up: NeMo Forced Aligner if you decide to move toward a CTC/Hybrid-CTC stack. The reason is practical: torchaudio’s CTC forced-alignment APIs entered maintenance/deprecation in 2.8 and are slated for removal in 2.9, which makes them a poor strategic dependency; NeMo Forced Aligner is actively documented, emits word-level CTM and even ASS outputs, and is the strongest “if we move away from WhisperX” alternative I could verify. I did not find a 2025–2026 aligner with a clearly demonstrated public superiority to WhisperX for your specific “Whisper-family transcript + editor-safe word timings” workflow. 

Native speaker-attributed word output in overlap.
Recommendation: do not plan around a native open model replacing your cascaded overlap-handling pipeline yet. Runner-up: research-only tracking of Sortformer-linked multi-speaker ASR, target-speaker overlap transcription, and newer joint “who spoke what” models such as SpeakerLM. The papers are encouraging, but the maturity gap is still large: what is state-of-the-art in papers is not yet the same thing as a robust, self-hostable, editor-facing system that gives stable speaker attribution, overlap handling, unknown-speaker continuity, and trustworthy word timings over noisy field audio. Switch worth it: no for production today; keep the cascaded design and treat joint speaker-attributed ASR as a watchlist item. 

Diarization and speaker identity
Open diarization for overlap-heavy real-world audio.
Recommendation: stay with pyannote speaker-diarization-community-1 as the open self-hosted baseline. Runner-up: Sortformer-family research only if you are explicitly prepared to run a research branch, not a straight production migration. The strongest directly relevant public evidence is pyannote’s own benchmark table, which is reported under the scoring regime you asked for: fully automatic processing, no forgiveness collar, and no skipping overlapping speech. On that basis, community-1 improves substantially over pyannote 3.1 across major sets including AliMeeting, AMI, AVA-AVD, DIHARD 3, MSDWild, and Ego4D, while remaining fully local and offline-capable. 

The research challengers are interesting but not yet sufficient to dethrone pyannote for your exact use case. Sortformer is a serious research line and clearly aimed at bridging diarization and ASR, but the public material I reviewed is still paper-first rather than “production-grade diarizer replacement” evidence. VibeVoice-ASR makes the most ambitious unified claim — ASR + diarization + timestamping in one long-context system — but what is public is still a technical report, not the kind of evaluative, maintenance-rich, deployment-ready package I would tell you to trust on unreleased film footage. Switch worth it versus community-1: no for self-hosted production; only-if you want a parallel R&D track. 

pyannote precision-2 API.
Recommendation: use precision-2 only if you are willing to accept hosted processing for measurable DER gains. Runner-up: keep community-1 and spend effort on fusion and speaker adjudication instead. The official pyannote benchmark table shows precision-2 ahead of community-1 on nearly every listed benchmark under the overlap-inclusive scoring regime: for example AliMeeting channel 1 drops from 20.3 to 15.2 DER, AMI IHM from 17.0 to 12.9, AMI SDM from 19.9 to 15.6, AVA-AVD from 44.6 to 37.1, CALLHOME from 26.7 to 16.6, DIHARD 3 from 20.2 to 14.7, MSDWild from 22.8 to 17.3, and VoxConverse from 11.2 to 8.5. That is not marginal. 

The data-handling posture is unusually explicit and is the key deciding factor for your footage. pyannote states that it never uses your audio, job outputs, or other customer data to train its AI models; URL-submitted audio is temporarily downloaded and not retained after processing, Media API uploads are deleted within 48 hours, job outputs are retained for 24 hours, and they offer self-hosted / on-prem enterprise options where audio and outputs remain in your own environment. Public documentation reviewed here does not expose a fixed universal price; pyannote’s billing docs say the dashboard is the source of truth for current per-unit rates. Switch worth it versus community-1: only-if hosted processing is acceptable; otherwise stay local. 

Speaker verification on short noisy clips.
Recommendation: if you are reopening this area, the most worthwhile challengers are ReDimNet2 and ECAPA2, with W2V-BERT2/MFA-style SSL-front-end systems as the high-ceiling heavy option. Runner-up: deeper WeSpeaker ResNet221/293-LM if you want the lowest-friction upgrade path from your current WeSpeaker tooling. Public evidence does support the idea that the field has moved beyond old “ResNet34-LM and stop” assumptions: WeSpeaker’s own VoxCeleb recipe shows deeper backbones improving materially over ResNet34, and ReDimNet2 reports 0.287% EER on Vox1-O at 12.3M parameters / 13 GMACs, while ECAPA2 explicitly targets short utterance lengths and overlapping speech robustness. 

That said, the exact benchmark you care about — 0.5–3 second, noisy, off-mic, field-recorded fragments — is still poorly standardised in public head-to-head reporting. So the clean research conclusion is not “model X is definitely best”, but “the old clean VoxCeleb leaderboard is not enough, and ECAPA2 / ReDimNet2 / strong SSL front-ends are now credible enough that not testing them would be a miss.” Your earlier uploaded ERF notes also suggest you were already feeling this pressure in practice, which lines up with the public trend. Switch worth it versus WeSpeaker ResNet34-LM: only-if, but this is one of the strongest cases for a fresh local bake-off. 
 

Face recognition and active speaker detection
Face stack.
Recommendation: keep InsightFace buffalo_l unless you have a specific reason to move to a supported commercial SDK. Runner-up: InspireFace if you want the same ecosystem’s newer SDK path rather than a fundamentally different open model family. The reason is not that face recognition research stopped; it is that InsightFace is still active in 2025–2026, shipped an InsightFace 1.0 update in May 2026, and remains one of the few mature, end-to-end, self-hostable projects spanning detection, recognition, and deployment tooling. The important caveat is licensing: the code is MIT, but the project explicitly notes model / training-data licensing restrictions, and its November 2025 update added direct contact points for licensing around open-sourced face recognition models such as buffalo_l. Switch worth it versus buffalo_l: no from a purely technical/productivity standpoint; just tidy the licensing story if commercial use matters. 

Active speaker detection.
Recommendation: keep Light-ASD as the default production ASD path. Runner-up: a UniTalk-era research branch built around LoCoNet or newer UniTalk-trained models if you decide ASD is still materially bottlenecking overlap attribution. The production case for Light-ASD is still strong: the original paper reports 94.1% mAP on AVA-ActiveSpeaker with dramatically smaller resource use, and the public repo reports 94.06% mAP on the AVA validation set, under an MIT licence, with an IJCV 2025 extension showing the line is still alive. By comparison, TalkNet remains a strong baseline but sits lower in the checked public repo results at 92.3 mAP on validation and 90.8 on test, and even its own README emphasises domain-transfer pain outside AVA. 

The reason I would not recommend a blind swap from Light-ASD is that more recent ASD work is telling a subtler story. LoCoNet pushed AVA mAP to 95.2% and reported much better results on Columbia, Talkies, and Ego4D; UniTalk then argued that AVA is too easy and too old-movie-biased, and that modern “in-the-wild” generalisation is still unsolved. That means the right question for ERF is not “what has the highest AVA number?” but “what best transfers to noisy, messy, crowded behind-the-scenes footage?” On the evidence reviewed, Light-ASD is still the best balance of accuracy, simplicity, size, and maintenance, and a UniTalk-era model is worth trying only if ASD is still visibly causing attribution misses after diarization + voiceprint fusion. Switch worth it versus Light-ASD: only-if you are prepared to retrain/evaluate on a more realistic benchmark setup. 

Hosted speech-layer alternatives
The honest answer is that a hosted API can plausibly beat parts of your self-hosted speech layer, but is unlikely to beat the whole ERF speech layer once enrolled-speaker identity, overlap adjudication, anonymous-stable unknown IDs, face fusion, and unreleased-footage constraints are included. Hosted vendors are strongest on raw transcript quality, turnkey diarization, and operational simplicity. Your stack remains stronger on privacy control, custom fusion, post-hoc adjudication, and project-specific identity logic. 
 

AssemblyAI.
Recommendation: only consider if you want a very easy benchmark target, not as an automatic replacement. Runner-up: none among hosted vendors if privacy is the primary concern. Public pricing is clear: Universal-2 is $0.15/hour and diarization is $0.02/hour, so roughly $8.50 for 50h or $17 for 100h; Universal-3.5 Pro is $0.21/hour plus the same diarization add-on. The platform advertises word-level timestamps and diarization, but its data policy is the catch for unreleased footage: AssemblyAI says certain submitted files may be used for model training unless you are on European servers, covered by a BAA, or have opted out. In async production, uploaded audio defaults to deletion processes beginning at 72 hours unless a shorter TTL is configured, and final transcription artifacts can persist up to 30 days in the non-TTL/no-BAA case. Switch worth it versus self-hosted: only-if you accept the retention/training controls and the convenience matters more than full custody. 

Deepgram.
Recommendation: strongest hosted price/performance candidate for a benchmark run. Runner-up: Speechmatics. Deepgram positions Nova-3 Monolingual as its highest-performing model for background noise, crosstalk, and far-field audio at $0.0048/min and diarization at $0.0020/min, so roughly $0.408/hour, $20.40 for 50h, or $40.80 for 100h on pay-as-you-go. It provides word-level word objects and diarization features in the docs. On data use, the pricing page explicitly says listed rates opt in to the Model Improvement Program; Deepgram’s MIP docs say opted-out requests can be excluded via mip_opt_out=true, and data from opted-out requests is retained only for the duration necessary to process the request. Switch worth it versus self-hosted: only-if you want a serious external baseline and will explicitly opt out of model improvement. 

Gladia.
Recommendation: best hosted option here if privacy posture matters and you still want turnkey STT. Runner-up: Deepgram. The public pricing page is unusually simple: Async $0.61/hour on Starter, as low as $0.20/hour on Growth, with diarization and word-level timestamps included in the plan comparison. More importantly for your footage, Gladia says on its pricing and compliance pages that paid plans provide data opt-out / zero data retention, that they never train on customer data by default, and that any training exception requires explicit agreement. If I had to pick one hosted vendor from the sources reviewed for “best combination of capability + data posture”, this is the safest answer. Switch worth it versus self-hosted: only-if you are comfortable with hosted processing at all; technically plausible, but not enough to erase the benefits of your custom fusion stack. 

Speechmatics.
Recommendation: very credible hosted benchmark target, especially if word timings and per-word speaker labels matter. Runner-up: Deepgram. Speechmatics’ public product page explicitly says it provides speaker labelling for each word and accurate timestamps for every word, and the pricing page puts Pro at from $0.129/hour with enterprise options for privacy-first deployment and on-prem / flexible deployment. Their pricing FAQ states that model training is enabled by an account setting, with discounted pricing while it is enabled, which implies it is a customer-controlled choice rather than a hard default in all cases. Their privacy policy says customer data is processed on the customer’s behalf to provide services, while R&D partner data is governed by separate agreements. Switch worth it versus self-hosted: only-if you want a hosted benchmark with a more enterprise-style deployment story; otherwise the gain over your custom pipeline is uncertain. 

ElevenLabs Scribe.
Recommendation: treat as a benchmark curiosity, not a primary migration target. Runner-up: none. The documentation is technically attractive — Scribe v2 advertises precise word-level timestamps, speaker diarization up to 32 speakers, dynamic audio tagging, and smart language detection — but the pricing model is credit-based, not clean per-audio-hour pricing. Using their own FAQ numbers, speech-to-text consumes 330 credits per minute; mapping that to the public Starter plan’s 30,000 credits for $6 implies an approximate effective STT rate near $3.96/hour on that subscription tier, but that is an inference, not a formally published “per hour STT” number. For enterprise, they advertise custom DPA/SLA terms and BAAs, but I did not verify a clear public retention/training statement for STT in the reviewed docs. Switch worth it versus self-hosted: no at current evidence level. 

Rev AI.
Recommendation: only use as a low-cost benchmark. Runner-up: none. Rev AI’s public pricing is straightforward — Reverb $0.20/hour, Reverb Turbo $0.10/hour, Forced Alignment $0.003/minute — and it exposes distinct STT and forced-alignment products, which is relevant to your pipeline shape. What I could not verify in the reviewed material was a matching level of public clarity around diarization and data-retention / model-training posture for unreleased footage. Switch worth it versus self-hosted: no unless your own test batch clearly surprises you. 

System shape
This section is necessarily more constraint-driven architecture judgement than benchmark-backed vendor ranking, because I stopped at the highest-confidence speech-layer research under the token cap. I am therefore being explicit about where I am inferring from your operating constraints rather than citing a fresh 2026 market bake-off. 

Orchestration.
Recommendation: keep the bespoke Python stages + SQLite journal shape, but harden it into a clearer asset catalogue contract rather than migrating whole-cloth to a heavier orchestrator. Runner-up: Dagster if you want one migration target that actually pays for itself with asset lineage, re-materialisation, and UI-first replay/debugging. Given your constraints — single operator, batch only, canonical file-backed JSON, and MooseFS with no safe concurrent writes to one file — the main value of Dagster / Prefect / Temporal / Snakemake is not “more distribution”; it is better state semantics, reproducibility, backfills, and observability. For ERF, those are real but not obviously worth the build-attention cost unless your current failure recovery and audit trail are becoming painful. Switch worth it versus bespoke: no for now; only-if you want UI lineage and deterministic re-runs badly enough to pay the migration tax. 

Retrieval.
Recommendation: conceptually keep the current recipe: hybrid lexical + dense retrieval + reranking, because that remains the right shape for a small, high-value editorial corpus. Runner-up: none until you finish a fresh embedder bake-off. I did not complete a benchmark-backed 2026 survey proving a clear replacement for LanceDB + SQLite FTS + Qwen3 8B embedders under your exact corpus size and multimodal-editing needs. So the best defensible answer here is category-level: the right thing to revisit is not “vector DB yes/no”; it is the embedder + reranker pair and your hybrid defaults. Switch worth it versus current retrieval stack: only-if a local bake-off shows materially better retrieval quality, not because the category is wrong. 

Auto-edit and render.
Recommendation: keep the bespoke ffmpeg/ASS render path. Runner-up: none strong enough to recommend replacing it sight unseen. For your output, determinism matters more than developer aesthetics: you need word-tight timings, per-speaker colour coding, burned captions, and batch reliability. That is exactly where ffmpeg/ASS remains disproportionately strong. Higher-level programmatic renderers are appealing when the bottleneck is layout creativity or browser-native motion design; your bottleneck is alignment fidelity and automation correctness. Switch worth it versus bespoke ffmpeg/ASS: no. 

Missing category.
The category-level addition I would make is not another model family; it is a formal evaluation / QA harness. Systems like yours benefit from a small but ruthless gold set covering the exact failure classes you care about: off-mic speech, overlapped speech, drop-outs, music underbeds, face-visible/non-speaking shots, face-offscreen speaking shots, one-off unknowns, and recurring cast with costume/location variation. Combine that with confidence-driven review queues at the word / segment / speaker-ID level and you get outsized gains without re-architecting everything. Several of the reviewed speech systems explicitly expose word-level confidences or confidence-enabled outputs, which makes this a natural missing layer. Switch worth it versus current category set: yes. 
 

Recommended next-state stack
If I had to freeze a July 2026 practical stack recommendation for ERF, it would be this:

Keep your overall system shape. Do not throw away the cascaded design. Replace components only where the public evidence and your constraints line up. For the open/self-hosted path, the most defensible “next test” stack is: Parakeet-TDT-0.6B-v3 as the main ASR challenger to faster-whisper, WhisperX retained as the alignment baseline unless a NeMo-CTC branch wins end-to-end, pyannote community-1 retained as the default self-hosted diarizer, ReDimNet2 / ECAPA2 / strong SSL-front-end speaker verification added to the bake-off pool, InsightFace buffalo_l retained, Light-ASD retained, and your existing ffmpeg/ASS and bespoke batch orchestration left intact. 

If you are willing to accept hosted processing for a benchmark or a partial layer swap, the most compelling shortlist from the reviewed sources is Gladia, Deepgram, and Speechmatics, in that order for your use case. Gladia looks best on explicit privacy posture; Deepgram looks strongest on public price/performance framing for noisy/crosstalk audio; Speechmatics looks strongest on “per-word speaker labels and enterprise deployment seriousness.” AssemblyAI is easy to test and cheap, but its training/retention posture requires more active management than I would want for unreleased film by default. Switch worth it overall versus the current self-hosted speech layer: no as a blanket move; only-if one hosted provider wins your local batch test by a margin large enough to offset the custody trade-off.
